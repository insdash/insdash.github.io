POST A — RECOMMENDED. 1834 chars (limit 3000).
First line is 141 chars, so it survives the "…see more" fold as a
complete thought. The hook is the contradiction: hired to replace

============================================================

A Zürich language school asked us to replace the scheduling software they were renting. The first thing we did was try to talk them out of it.

Six rooms. Six teachers. Two owners. They were paying a subscription priced for an organisation many times their size, and using a fraction of it. When the price went up, renewing meant paying more for capacity they would never touch. Not renewing meant no scheduling at all.

So we counted. How many rooms, how many people, which of the product's features appeared in a typical week, and what would break if each one disappeared. Then we costed the alternatives over three years — including the part most estimates leave out, which is what it costs to keep a custom system alive after it ships.

At this size, building won. The requirement was small, stable and unlikely to grow: a school with six rooms in one building does not wake up needing multi-site resource planning.

It is worth saying when it would not have won. Had they been opening locations, or had the timetable needed to talk to payroll and invoicing, the answer would have been to keep renting. Build-vs-buy is a judgment about a specific business at a specific size, not a preference.

One month from signed scope to production. Live since July. They own it outright — the repository and the accounts are theirs, and we hold the maintenance contract.

Then in August they asked us for a native app.

We told them not to buy one. What they actually wanted was to reach the schedule quickly from a phone, and that is a much smaller thing than an app. They got a home-screen icon instead: two lines of code in place of a project we would have been paid to build.

The savings from leaving a subscription are only real if the people who replaced it are still willing to talk you out of spending.

POST B — SHORTER, ONE IDEA. 1242 chars.
Use if A feels long, or as the second post in a sequence.
============================================================

A client asked us to build them a native app. We told them not to buy one.

They are a language school in Zürich. Six rooms, six teachers, eight people in total. We had replaced their scheduling subscription in July with something they own outright, and in August they came back wanting an app on top of it.

A native build would have added push notifications, offline access, and a window with no browser chrome. For eight people checking which room is free at four o'clock, none of the three was the need. What they actually wanted was to reach the schedule quickly from a phone.

So they got an icon. We drew the mark, wired it in, and showed them how to put the schedule on their home screen — two lines of code in place of a project we would have been paid to build.

We had spent the spring making the case that leaving their subscription would save them money. That case does not survive us selling them the next unnecessary thing in August.

The savings from leaving rented software are only real if the people who replaced it are still willing to talk you out of spending.

Screens are the delivered software running on a demonstration organisation — invented teachers, invented bookings, no school data.

POST C — THE DEFECT. 1889 chars.

============================================================

We shipped a defect that quietly added hours to people's timesheets. Here is what it was and what we did about it.

We built a scheduling system for a Zürich language school this summer, replacing a subscription priced for an organisation many times their size. The second release added an hours report — lessons and hours per teacher over any date range, which is the number the school invoices on.

A term of weekly classes has holidays in it, and taking one out meant deleting those lessons from the series. That did not hold. Change anything about the series afterwards — nudge its end date, move its time — and every deleted lesson quietly came back.

Nothing on screen said so. The hours simply went up, for a teacher who had not touched a thing. A defect in software we delivered, sitting directly under the figures that decide what people get paid, and the kind that never throws an error for anyone to report.

The fix went at the cause rather than the symptom. A holiday is now part of the term: choose Skip dates, give it a from and a to, and the gap is written into the series rather than cut out of it. No later edit can fill it back in. The button says Skip this date rather than Delete, because that is what it does.

The harder call was what not to repair. We did not convert the gaps made before the fix. A date missing from a term might have been deleted, or its lesson might have been moved to another day, and the data cannot tell those two apart. Converting them would have meant rewriting a live timetable on a guess. The release changed no booking that was already there.

Software a business runs its week on needs someone still answering in year two. This is what that looks like on an ordinary Tuesday.

Screens are the delivered software running on a demonstration organisation — invented teachers, invented bookings, no school data.

  body get less distribution. Post, then immediately comment:
  https://insdash.ch/projects/sprachschule-yang/

  Company pages have thin organic reach on their own; the personal
  reshare is what moves it.

  front of their network, which is the right audience, and it is their
